\title{Certifying the Collateral Cost of Intrusion Response in Sliced 5G User Planes: Direct Risk Control Versus False-Positive Control}
\author{[Author names and affiliations to be supplied]}
\markboth{IEEE Transactions on Network and Service Management (submitted)}{Certifying Collateral Cost of Slice-Aware Intrusion Response}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Mitigating a flagged user equipment by rate-limiting or blocking it
harms benign users whenever the detector errs, and in a sliced 5G user
plane the harm depends on the slice and the action. Existing calibrated
intrusion detectors certify alert-level false-positive rates, and
intrusion-response methods offer only model-based guarantees. We ask
whether certifying each slice's action-induced service-level-agreement
(SLA) violation risk directly admits more attack mitigation than
certifying each slice's false-positive rate at equal budgets and
confidence. We define collateral risk against the no-action
counterfactual and prove that, under a monotone scheduler, it is bounded
by the per-slice acted-benign fraction. Per-slice false-positive
certification is therefore also a collateral certificate, and any gain
from direct certification must come from the ratio between the two. We
certify 1331 per-slice rate-limit/block policies with Learn-then-Test
risk control on replayed counterfactual outcomes, and evaluate in a
fluid simulator of a three-slice egress link whose detector is deployed
under domain shift. Over 200 calibration/test splits, no certified
method exceeded its budgets, while empirically constrained selection did
in 25.5\% of splits with 100 calibration episodes. With fixed-sequence
testing, direct certification delivered 0.417 of attack traffic versus
0.458 for false-positive certification, winning in 93.5\% of paired
splits. The advantage vanished with small calibration sets, other budget
levels, or Bonferroni control. Direct certification pays off only where
harm ratios are well below one.
\end{abstract}
